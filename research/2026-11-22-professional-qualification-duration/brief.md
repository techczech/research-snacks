# Provisional brief

## Purpose, reader and genre

This is a decision report for the person commissioning a Research Snack. They know that familiar national education routes differ and want to test whether familiar minimum durations are necessary. They need an inventory of usable sources, the differences that could invalidate a comparison, evidence on both sides, and a choice of feasible snack shapes. They do not need a finished entry or deployment. The report speaks as the researching agent, never in the author's first person.

The report proceeds from source feasibility to comparison rules, then contrary evidence and possible outputs. Every empirical proposition needs a source or an explicit unverified label. Tables compare source coverage and candidate outputs; they do not rank practitioner quality. No quantitative world comparison is being generated during scoping.

## Settled instructions

- Scope only. Do not build, deploy or publish a snack.
- Include Asian systems as distinct examples, not a single Asian tradition.
- Look for evidence both for and against the conventional-minimum hypothesis.
- Distinguish degree length from complete routes to independent practice, including supervised stages.
- Check the learning log and dataset catalogue first. Return new catalogue entries as drafts to the coordinator; do not edit the catalogue.
- Commit and push the scoping records. No private conversation, account information or machine details belong in the public repository.
- A snack requires an author preview before going live. Approval of this scope would not be approval to publish.

## Not yet settled

The precise output, included jurisdictions, profession-specific endpoint and research time budget remain the author's decisions. No author interview answers or approved conclusion have been inferred from the relayed brief.

## Author interview

The first question is which output to pursue, informed by the source audit. The agent recommends a bounded route comparison with a separate evidence section on competence. The report states the alternatives and their limits. Later questions should be asked one at a time: which observation would change the author's view; which endpoints matter; which outcome evidence they would accept; whether they have additional sources; how much review time they can give; and which wording may be attributed to them.
