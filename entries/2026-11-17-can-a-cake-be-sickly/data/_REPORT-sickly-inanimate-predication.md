# Sickly with food and other inanimate referents

## Findings at a glance

**The evidence supports both attributive and predicative _sickly_ with food and other inanimate referents. Coordination is optional.** Cakes, chocolate and drinks occur in both positions; a supplementary web passage even describes the same cake first attributively and then predicatively. The relevant contrast is where the adjective functions, rather than whether _sickly_ is an adjective at all.

The most useful generalisation concerns **the property being evaluated**. Food and drink can be cloying, excessively rich or unpleasantly intense. Food can also look unhealthy: the ham example concerns appearance. Light and colour evoke an unhealthy visual impression; economies and cultural forms can evoke weakness. These are contextual readings, not a demonstrated taxonomy of nouns that permits or blocks a construction.

**The available American evidence does not settle a British–American difference in food use.** The Open American National Corpus contains only twelve written and one spoken exact-form occurrences. It supplies shared attributive visual and economic uses, but no clear food evaluation in this sample. That absence cannot establish an American grammatical prohibition.

This report combines national-corpus concordances, automatically extracted grammatical relations, association measures, and supplementary historical web attestations. It presents positive evidence for constructions separately from frequency claims. The interactive tables retain raw counts and source identifiers so that an unusual high-scoring pair does not become a presumed rule of grammar.

## Questions and construction types

The investigation asks whether the nouns following _sickly_ differ from the referents described by predicative _sickly_, whether particular food classes favour one position, and whether another coordinated adjective is required. “Sickly as the only noun” in the original question is interpreted here as **sickly as the only adjective**.

The following schematic examples are constructed to explain the distinction; they are not additional corpus attestations.

| Construction | Schematic example | What is being analysed |
|---|---|---|
| Attributive, sole adjective | a sickly cake | _Sickly_ modifies the noun inside its noun phrase. |
| Attributive, coordinated | sweet and sickly cakes | Two adjectives share the noun as their target. |
| Subject predication, sole adjective | the cake is sickly | _Sickly_ predicates a property of the subject. |
| Subject predication, coordinated | the cake is sweet and sickly | Two coordinated adjectives predicate properties of the same subject. |
| Object predication | I found the drink sickly | The adjective predicates a property of the object. |
| Adjective modification | a sickly sweet cake / the cake is sickly sweet | _Sickly_ modifies _sweet_; this is not coordination and does not establish _sickly_ as the sole predicative adjective. |
| Predicative noun phrase | the toothpaste is a sickly green | The whole noun phrase is predicative; _sickly_ is attributive within it. |
| Ellipsis or supplement | quite sickly / the ham pale and sickly | Recoverable predication without an overt full copular clause at that point. |

A phrase such as _a bit sickly_ contains a degree expression, not a second adjective. Adjacent _sickly sweet_ and coordinated _sweet and sickly_ therefore belong to different columns of the analysis. This distinction matters particularly for biscuits and brownies, where adjective-modifying examples otherwise inflate the apparent evidence for direct noun modification.

### What the context resolves

The BNC chocolate-cake example follows _look_, and the ham passage compares appearance with taste. Both concern consumable referents, but neither warrants automatically coding the adjective as “too sugary”. The canned-tomato passage concerns excessive use, giving a food evaluation beyond desserts. The cherry-brandy passage links the evaluation to an initially enjoyable sweet drink that becomes nauseating.

Colour and light have their own sensory basis. The art-writing colour example quotes an American critic inside a British corpus document: it proves occurrence in the BNC, not exclusively British authorship. The economy and parody examples extend bodily weakness to institutions or cultural forms. Plants and seedlings can be literally unhealthy; those cases were excluded from the non-living food comparison.

## Collocation measures: neighbouring words

The **window tables** describe lemma collocates within five tokens to the left or right of an exact-form _sickly_ node. They combine all POS analyses of the node. No additional sentence-boundary restriction was set. A neighbouring lemma is not necessarily a noun modified by _sickly_, and repeated windows can come from the same passage.

The settings were: corpus-wide candidate frequency at least 5; window co-occurrence count at least 2; up to 100 candidates; descending logDice. The BNC response reached the 100-row cap. Its self-collocate _sickly_ has been removed, leaving 99 displayed rows. The OANC written response contained 13 rows. Consequently, the BNC table is a **ranked, truncated retrieval**, not every word appearing in the concordance windows. Missing rows can reflect thresholds or the cap.

Each row gives **fAB**, the returned window co-occurrence count, and **fB**, the collocate’s corpus-wide lemma frequency. Counts are occurrences, not independent documents or speakers. Values are the engine’s returned statistics, displayed to three decimals; CSV export retains the stored precision.

### British window collocations

{{BNC_COLLOCATIONS}}

Three measures answer different questions. **MI** compares observed with expected co-occurrence and can favour rare combinations. **T-score** is more responsive to repeated occurrence. **LogDice** scales overlap against the frequencies of the two items without an explicit corpus-size term. None is a probability that a construction is grammatical. The engine’s [MI](https://www.sketchengine.eu/glossary/mi-score/) and [T-score](https://www.sketchengine.eu/glossary/t-score/) explanations describe their frequency sensitivities.

The distinction is visible in the BNC. _Stench_ occurs only **3** times in the window table but has logDice **7.675** and MI **12.521**. _Sweet_ occurs **15** times, with logDice **6.726**, MI **10.770** and T-score **3.871**. _Pale_ occurs **13** times and _smell_ **11**. The higher score of a rare item does not mean it is the most frequent companion. The profile supports sensory and ill-health associations; the KWIC determines which interpretation applies to an individual food or noun.

### American window collocations

Only twelve written nodes underlie these scores. _Likely_ and _child_ each have a co-occurrence count of **2**. In particular, the two _sickly_ occurrences in a single mother-and-baby sentence create overlapping windows around _likely_; this is not replication across independent texts. The table is included to expose the sparsity rather than conceal it.

### How the scores are defined

For the window calculation, let fA be the node frequency, fB the candidate frequency, fAB the co-occurrence count, and N the engine’s corpus token count. The conventional engine formulas are:

- **MI = log₂(fAB × N / (fA × fB)).**
- **T-score = (fAB − fA × fB / N) / √fAB.**
- **LogDice = 14 + log₂(2 × fAB / (fA + fB)).**

The displayed window logDice values were checked against the returned counts. The BNC has N = 112,338,376 tokens; OANC writing has N = 13,572,382. Word-sketch logDice instead uses grammatical-relation marginals and therefore has a different counting basis. The two logDice tables are not interchangeable. See the engine’s [statistics specification](https://www.sketchengine.eu/wp-content/uploads/ske-statistics.pdf).

## Grammatical collocations: nouns and coordination

Word sketches extract **grammatical relations**, rather than all nearby words. The BNC adjective sketch reports 137 noun-modification relations. The table below retains the engine’s per-pair counts and logDice scores, including one-occurrence pairs. Filtering to a minimum count of two reveals the repeated associations. High singleton scores describe a rare pair’s association; they do not establish a productive preference.

_Child_ is the most frequent retrieved modified noun (**15**), followed by _smell_ (**10**). Among repeated inanimate sensory heads are _green_ (**4**), _stench_, _sweetness_, _hue_, _white_, _colour_ and _light_ (**2** each). Food-related heads include _dessert_, _sweet_, _mix_, _button_ and _drink_, each with a count of **1** in this grammatical extraction. Their rarity limits ranking by noun class. Compound heads are represented by their head noun: the shortbread example is indexed as _button_, not a separate _shortbread_ category.

The extraction is a discovery aid. It includes possible tagging or parsing errors and noun senses that require context. It does not exhaust all attributive examples: coordinated phrases, nominal colour terms and compound structure can change what is detected. The sketch’s four “subjects of be sickly” relations likewise do not equal four verified standalone predicates; one involves _something_ with postpositive modification. They are not used as a numerator in an attributive-versus-predicative ratio.

### Coordinated adjectives

{{COORD_SKETCH}}

The automated “and/or” relation reports 86 extracted relations, with _sweet_ and _pale_ at **9** each, _weak_ at **4**, _poor_ at **3**, and several rare pairs. This relation is broader than a manually verified `[adjective] and sickly` query. It can include reversed order, other conjunctions and extraction errors; its total is not a count of food coordination or of one exact surface pattern.

The concordances answer the requirement question more directly. **Another adjective is not required:** the food annotation contains both sole and coordinated _sickly_ in attributive and explicit predicative positions. Where the examples coordinate adjectives, the expressions share a local syntactic role and referent. Comma-separated sequences, such as the soy-sauce description, are labelled asyndetic / juxtaposed in the KWIC rather than counted as an explicit _and sickly_ construction. The partner supplies another property; it need not be _sweet_, a synonym, or a negative judgement. The BNC light passage coordinates a favourable style judgement with an unfavourable sensory judgement. A relation score measures association with a partner, not a condition licensing _sickly_.

## Matched nouns, food kinds and biscuits

### Cake: the same referent in both positions

The enTenTen13 interview passage at Culture Compass contains a direct attributive description and a later predicative reaction to **the same cake**. Both use _sickly_ without a coordinated adjective. The paired rows in the KWIC table are the strongest local answer to an attributive-only account of food _sickly_: noun identity and discourse context are held closely constant. This is an attestation, not an experiment or frequency comparison.

Chocolate and drinks also have both constructions across different texts. The supplementary web set supplies attributive chocolates and cherry brandy; the BNC supplies predicative evaluations of those referent types. These comparisons hold the general food type constant, not the physical item, author or genre. ukWaC adds standalone predication of cream and white chocolate.

### A perceived property, not a food taxonomy

The inspected web candidates include cakes, chocolates, frosting, syrup, sauces and drinks. Sweet or rich preparations recur, but the canned-tomato and soy-sauce cases widen the interpretation beyond sugar. Ham shows that food can receive a visual evaluation. The evidence is compatible with an unpleasant or excessive sensory effect whose source varies with the referent and context.

There is no demonstrated rule that baked goods are attributive while drinks are predicative, or that solid/liquid status governs the construction. Establishing a preference by food class would require systematic annotation and suitable denominators for each class and construction. The present targeted retrieval is evidence for availability and plausible semantic groupings, not that statistical comparison.

### Cookies and biscuits: what was actually retrieved

The completed enTenTen13 query `[lc="sickly"] [lemma="cookie|biscuit"]` returned **zero** adjacent matches. A second query admitting intervening adjective tags and the compound _sickly-sweet_ returned **two** candidates. One describes actual biscuits using _sickly sweet_; the other compares a cosmetic’s smell with a biscuit. They appear in the KWIC table as adjective-modifying examples, with the cosmetic comparison separately labelled.

No cookie token is supplied here as an attestation, and no completed count of cookie predicates was obtained. Zero results for a narrow adjacent query do not establish that bare _sickly biscuits_ or _the cookies are sickly_ is unacceptable. Intervening words, different noun forms, pronoun subjects, corpus coverage and query failure all matter. The shortbread-button example establishes direct attributive use for a related baked food, but it does not by itself establish a biscuit-specific or cookie-specific distribution.

## What the American comparison can establish

Manual inspection of all twelve written OANC occurrences identified **eleven attributive tokens and one predicative token**, the latter describing children. The single spoken occurrence is attributive and describes kids. The written sample contains visual descriptions of pallor, toothpaste colour and image appearance, plus an economic metaphor. A heroine’s state is an abstract head with a human-health referent, illustrating why “inanimate noun” and “non-living referent” are not identical categories.

All twelve written tokens were tagged **RB** in this corpus version, including clear adjective uses. The automatic American sketch therefore selected an adverb analysis and returned no grammatical relations. The empty sketch is a tagging artefact, not evidence that the adjective or construction is absent. Exact word-form retrieval avoided the POS gate; syntax was read manually.

These data support shared British and American attributive extensions into visual and economic domains. They do not establish a difference in standalone predicative food use. The British data positively attest the construction; the small American sample leaves its frequency and distribution unresolved. Corpus membership also does not guarantee the nationality of every quoted writer.

## Method, limits and reproducibility

The report was prepared with Codex from Sketch Engine API responses. Example interpretation and coding were produced from wider corpus contexts; this is not an independent human annotation study. The archived concordance queries preserve token positions, source references, wide context and completion indicators. National-corpus queries were completed, returning 218 BNC, twelve written OANC and one spoken OANC hits. No full BNC construction census was produced. The food subset and curated display are manually interpreted selections.

Window scores were retrieved through the documented `collx` endpoint. The exact configuration was:

```json
{
  "q": ["q[word=\"(?i)sickly\"]"],
  "cattr": "lemma",
  "cfromw": "-5", "ctow": "5",
  "cminfreq": "5", "cminbgr": "2",
  "cmaxitems": "100",
  "cbgrfns": ["t", "m", "d"],
  "csortfn": "d"
}
```

The corpus parameter selects `preloaded/bnc2_tt31` or `preloaded/oanc_text`. The BNC adjective word sketch requested expanded candidate lists with minimum frequency one and minimum score zero. Its per-word score is used here, not the summary statistic attached to an entire grammatical relation. The official [API documentation](https://www.sketchengine.eu/apidoc/) and [method documentation](https://www.sketchengine.eu/documentation/methods-documentation/) describe these interfaces.

Supplementary food searches were finite candidate searches, followed by contextual inspection. A query containing _sweet_ as a possible noun also matched adjectival _sweet_, so its raw total was not treated as a food-noun frequency. Non-food candidates included seedlings, cream as a colour, figurative syrup or icing, misleading token sequences and spam. Queries from nouns towards a predicate also miss food antecedents expressed later through pronouns.

The newer English web corpus and some broader searches timed out. Those attempts supplied **no negative evidence**. Only completed retrievals support reported zero counts. The historical web passages are attested in archived corpus contexts; their original pages were not independently verified as live during this investigation.

The offline report embeds the narrative, curated KWIC, collocation tables and source links. Its CSV buttons export the table rows currently visible after filtering. The stored data retain source identifiers and token positions; the wider original concordances remain in the research archive, rather than being represented as the curated table. The document contains no account credentials or live API dependency.
