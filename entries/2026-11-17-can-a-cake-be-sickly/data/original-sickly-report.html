Findings at a glance

The evidence supports both attributive and predicative sickly with food and other inanimate referents. Coordination is optional. Cakes, chocolate and drinks occur in both positions; a supplementary web passage even describes the same cake first attributively and then predicatively. The relevant contrast is where the adjective functions, rather than whether sickly is an adjective at all.

The most useful generalisation concerns the property being evaluated. Food and drink can be cloying, excessively rich or unpleasantly intense. Food can also look unhealthy: the ham example concerns appearance. Light and colour evoke an unhealthy visual impression; economies and cultural forms can evoke weakness. These are contextual readings, not a demonstrated taxonomy of nouns that permits or blocks a construction.

The available American evidence does not settle a British–American difference in food use. The Open American National Corpus contains only twelve written and one spoken exact-form occurrences. It supplies shared attributive visual and economic uses, but no clear food evaluation in this sample. That absence cannot establish an American grammatical prohibition.

This report combines national-corpus concordances, automatically extracted grammatical relations, association measures, and supplementary historical web attestations. It presents positive evidence for constructions separately from frequency claims. The interactive tables retain raw counts and source identifiers so that an unusual high-scoring pair does not become a presumed rule of grammar.

Questions and construction types

The investigation asks whether the nouns following sickly differ from the referents described by predicative sickly, whether particular food classes favour one position, and whether another coordinated adjective is required. “Sickly as the only noun” in the original question is interpreted here as sickly as the only adjective.

The following schematic examples are constructed to explain the distinction; they are not additional corpus attestations.

Construction Schematic example What is being analysed
Attributive, sole adjective a sickly cake Sickly modifies the noun inside its noun phrase.
Attributive, coordinated sweet and sickly cakes Two adjectives share the noun as their target.
Subject predication, sole adjective the cake is sickly Sickly predicates a property of the subject.
Subject predication, coordinated the cake is sweet and sickly Two coordinated adjectives predicate properties of the same subject.
Object predication I found the drink sickly The adjective predicates a property of the object.
Adjective modification a sickly sweet cake / the cake is sickly sweet Sickly modifies sweet; this is not coordination and does not establish sickly as the sole predicative adjective.
Predicative noun phrase the toothpaste is a sickly green The whole noun phrase is predicative; sickly is attributive within it.
Ellipsis or supplement quite sickly / the ham pale and sickly Recoverable predication without an overt full copular clause at that point.

A phrase such as a bit sickly contains a degree expression, not a second adjective. Adjacent sickly sweet and coordinated sweet and sickly therefore belong to different columns of the analysis. This distinction matters particularly for biscuits and brownies, where adjective-modifying examples otherwise inflate the apparent evidence for direct noun modification.

What the context resolves

The BNC chocolate-cake example follows look, and the ham passage compares appearance with taste. Both concern consumable referents, but neither warrants automatically coding the adjective as “too sugary”. The canned-tomato passage concerns excessive use, giving a food evaluation beyond desserts. The cherry-brandy passage links the evaluation to an initially enjoyable sweet drink that becomes nauseating.

Colour and light have their own sensory basis. The art-writing colour example quotes an American critic inside a British corpus document: it proves occurrence in the BNC, not exclusively British authorship. The economy and parody examples extend bodily weakness to institutions or cultural forms. Plants and seedlings can be literally unhealthy; those cases were excluded from the non-living food comparison.

Collocation measures: neighbouring words

The window tables describe lemma collocates within five tokens to the left or right of an exact-form sickly node. They combine all POS analyses of the node. No additional sentence-boundary restriction was set. A neighbouring lemma is not necessarily a noun modified by sickly, and repeated windows can come from the same passage.

The settings were: corpus-wide candidate frequency at least 5; window co-occurrence count at least 2; up to 100 candidates; descending logDice. The BNC response reached the 100-row cap. Its self-collocate sickly has been removed, leaving 99 displayed rows. The OANC written response contained 13 rows. Consequently, the BNC table is a ranked, truncated retrieval, not every word appearing in the concordance windows. Missing rows can reflect thresholds or the cap.

Each row gives fAB, the returned window co-occurrence count, and fB, the collocate’s corpus-wide lemma frequency. Counts are occurrences, not independent documents or speakers. Values are the engine’s returned statistics, displayed to three decimals; CSV export retains the stored precision.

Three measures answer different questions. MI compares observed with expected co-occurrence and can favour rare combinations. T-score is more responsive to repeated occurrence. LogDice scales overlap against the frequencies of the two items without an explicit corpus-size term. None is a probability that a construction is grammatical. The engine’s MI and T-score explanations describe their frequency sensitivities.

The distinction is visible in the BNC. Stench occurs only 3 times in the window table but has logDice 7.675 and MI 12.521. Sweet occurs 15 times, with logDice 6.726, MI 10.770 and T-score 3.871. Pale occurs 13 times and smell 11. The higher score of a rare item does not mean it is the most frequent companion. The profile supports sensory and ill-health associations; the KWIC determines which interpretation applies to an individual food or noun.

How the scores are defined

For the window calculation, let fA be the node frequency, fB the candidate frequency, fAB the co-occurrence count, and N the engine’s corpus token count. The conventional engine formulas are:

  • MI = log₂(fAB × N / (fA × fB)).
  • T-score = (fAB − fA × fB / N) / √fAB.
  • LogDice = 14 + log₂(2 × fAB / (fA + fB)).

The displayed window logDice values were checked against the returned counts. The BNC has N = 112,338,376 tokens; OANC writing has N = 13,572,382. Word-sketch logDice instead uses grammatical-relation marginals and therefore has a different counting basis. The two logDice tables are not interchangeable. See the engine’s statistics specification.

The automated “and/or” relation reports 86 extracted relations, with sweet and pale at 9 each, weak at 4, poor at 3, and several rare pairs. This relation is broader than a manually verified [adjective] and sickly query. It can include reversed order, other conjunctions and extraction errors; its total is not a count of food coordination or of one exact surface pattern.

The concordances answer the requirement question more directly. Another adjective is not required: the food annotation contains both sole and coordinated sickly in attributive and explicit predicative positions. Where the examples coordinate adjectives, the expressions share a local syntactic role and referent. Comma-separated sequences, such as the soy-sauce description, are labelled asyndetic / juxtaposed in the KWIC rather than counted as an explicit and sickly construction. The partner supplies another property; it need not be sweet, a synonym, or a negative judgement. The BNC light passage coordinates a favourable style judgement with an unfavourable sensory judgement. A relation score measures association with a partner, not a condition licensing sickly.

Matched nouns, food kinds and biscuits

Cake: the same referent in both positions

The enTenTen13 interview passage at Culture Compass contains a direct attributive description and a later predicative reaction to the same cake. Both use sickly without a coordinated adjective. The paired rows in the KWIC table are the strongest local answer to an attributive-only account of food sickly: noun identity and discourse context are held closely constant. This is an attestation, not an experiment or frequency comparison.

Chocolate and drinks also have both constructions across different texts. The supplementary web set supplies attributive chocolates and cherry brandy; the BNC supplies predicative evaluations of those referent types. These comparisons hold the general food type constant, not the physical item, author or genre. ukWaC adds standalone predication of cream and white chocolate.

A perceived property, not a food taxonomy

The inspected web candidates include cakes, chocolates, frosting, syrup, sauces and drinks. Sweet or rich preparations recur, but the canned-tomato and soy-sauce cases widen the interpretation beyond sugar. Ham shows that food can receive a visual evaluation. The evidence is compatible with an unpleasant or excessive sensory effect whose source varies with the referent and context.

There is no demonstrated rule that baked goods are attributive while drinks are predicative, or that solid/liquid status governs the construction. Establishing a preference by food class would require systematic annotation and suitable denominators for each class and construction. The present targeted retrieval is evidence for availability and plausible semantic groupings, not that statistical comparison.

Cookies and biscuits: what was actually retrieved

The completed enTenTen13 query [lc="sickly"] [lemma="cookie|biscuit"] returned zero adjacent matches. A second query admitting intervening adjective tags and the compound sickly-sweet returned two candidates. One describes actual biscuits using sickly sweet; the other compares a cosmetic’s smell with a biscuit. They appear in the KWIC table as adjective-modifying examples, with the cosmetic comparison separately labelled.

No cookie token is supplied here as an attestation, and no completed count of cookie predicates was obtained. Zero results for a narrow adjacent query do not establish that bare sickly biscuits or the cookies are sickly is unacceptable. Intervening words, different noun forms, pronoun subjects, corpus coverage and query failure all matter. The shortbread-button example establishes direct attributive use for a related baked food, but it does not by itself establish a biscuit-specific or cookie-specific distribution.

What the American comparison can establish

Manual inspection of all twelve written OANC occurrences identified eleven attributive tokens and one predicative token, the latter describing children. The single spoken occurrence is attributive and describes kids. The written sample contains visual descriptions of pallor, toothpaste colour and image appearance, plus an economic metaphor. A heroine’s state is an abstract head with a human-health referent, illustrating why “inanimate noun” and “non-living referent” are not identical categories.

All twelve written tokens were tagged RB in this corpus version, including clear adjective uses. The automatic American sketch therefore selected an adverb analysis and returned no grammatical relations. The empty sketch is a tagging artefact, not evidence that the adjective or construction is absent. Exact word-form retrieval avoided the POS gate; syntax was read manually.

These data support shared British and American attributive extensions into visual and economic domains. They do not establish a difference in standalone predicative food use. The British data positively attest the construction; the small American sample leaves its frequency and distribution unresolved. Corpus membership also does not guarantee the nationality of every quoted writer.

Method, limits and reproducibility

The report was prepared with Codex from Sketch Engine API responses. Example interpretation and coding were produced from wider corpus contexts; this is not an independent human annotation study. The archived concordance queries preserve token positions, source references, wide context and completion indicators. National-corpus queries were completed, returning 218 BNC, twelve written OANC and one spoken OANC hits. No full BNC construction census was produced. The food subset and curated display are manually interpreted selections.

Window scores were retrieved through the documented collx endpoint. The exact configuration was:

{
  "q": ["q[word=\"(?i)sickly\"]"],
  "cattr": "lemma",
  "cfromw": "-5", "ctow": "5",
  "cminfreq": "5", "cminbgr": "2",
  "cmaxitems": "100",
  "cbgrfns": ["t", "m", "d"],
  "csortfn": "d"
}

The corpus parameter selects preloaded/bnc2_tt31 or preloaded/oanc_text. The BNC adjective word sketch requested expanded candidate lists with minimum frequency one and minimum score zero. Its per-word score is used here, not the summary statistic attached to an entire grammatical relation. The official API documentation and method documentation describe these interfaces.

Supplementary food searches were finite candidate searches, followed by contextual inspection. A query containing sweet as a possible noun also matched adjectival sweet, so its raw total was not treated as a food-noun frequency. Non-food candidates included seedlings, cream as a colour, figurative syrup or icing, misleading token sequences and spam. Queries from nouns towards a predicate also miss food antecedents expressed later through pronouns.

The newer English web corpus and some broader searches timed out. Those attempts supplied no negative evidence. Only completed retrievals support reported zero counts. The historical web passages are attested in archived corpus contexts; their original pages were not independently verified as live during this investigation.

The offline report embeds the narrative, curated KWIC, collocation tables and source links. Its CSV buttons export the table rows currently visible after filtering. The stored data retain source identifiers and token positions; the wider original concordances remain in the research archive, rather than being represented as the curated table. The document contains no account credentials or live API dependency.
